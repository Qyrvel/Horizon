# Evaluation goal

Evaluate the public importance, urgency, scale, and reliability of global affairs and world news. The audience wants a concise but balanced understanding of what is happening around the world, why it matters, and what may change next.

# Scoring rubric

- **9-10: Critical global event.** Major war escalation or ceasefire, invasion, coup, major terrorist attack, severe humanitarian emergency, internationally significant outbreak, major election or constitutional crisis, or a decision with immediate and far-reaching consequences.
- **7-8: High importance.** Significant diplomatic or military development, consequential government action, major civil unrest, important court or election result, disaster with substantial impact, credible public-health warning, or a major human-rights development.
- **5-6: Worth knowing.** Meaningful regional development, policy movement, developing investigation, or public-health update with moderate impact or uncertain wider consequences.
- **3-4: Low priority.** Routine political statements, minor incidents, repetitive updates without material change, or narrow local stories with limited wider significance.
- **0-2: Noise or unreliable.** Clickbait, unsupported allegations, propaganda presented as fact, recycled stories with no new information, or opinion without substantive reporting.

# Evaluation guidance

Assess:
- Human impact: deaths, injuries, displacement, affected population, and disruption to essential services.
- Scale and reach: local, national, regional, or international consequences.
- Urgency and change: what is newly confirmed, what materially changed, and whether the situation is escalating or stabilizing.
- Evidence quality: named sources, independently corroborated reporting, official documents, credible humanitarian organizations, and transparent uncertainty.
- Context: relevant history and the positions of key parties, without treating any party's claims as established fact.

For conflict reporting, distinguish independently verified events from claims made by governments, militaries, armed groups, and other parties. Do not infer responsibility, casualty figures, territorial control, or motives when sources do not establish them. Attribute contested figures and claims.

For disease and public-health reporting, distinguish confirmed cases from suspected cases, outbreaks from isolated cases, and risk assessments from confirmed spread. Prefer statements from WHO and relevant public-health authorities, while noting when independent confirmation is unavailable.

Do not score a story highly merely because its headline is dramatic or because it is repeated by many outlets. Avoid double-counting updates that add no material information.

Use three to five specific topic tags.
